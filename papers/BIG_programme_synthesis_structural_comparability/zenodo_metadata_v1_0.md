## Description
This programme-level synthesis integrates the evidence from BIG-B19 through BIG-B26, including the B19E external-validation branch and the B23A structural-universality audit. The paper distinguishes structural, formal, and quantitative universality and preserves positive, null, inconclusive, implementation-invalid, and transfer-failure outcomes as part of the evidential record rather than retrospectively regrading them.

B19 did not support a robust ray-independent upstream scalar collapse in the tested synthetic vorticity families. B19E subsequently found no prospectively established incremental predictive information from the frozen BIG additions beyond a strong external baseline in its primary holdout (Delta R^2 = -0.00284577; hierarchical 95% interval [-0.01016985, 0.00421586]), while a separate finite-control-volume local-budget audit closed the resolved operator bookkeeping to near machine precision without rescuing that predictive result. B20-B22 nevertheless established useful finite-resolution local response geometry and sequential held-out prediction within restricted frozen branches and subspaces. B23 retained an INCONCLUSIVE original aggregate verdict despite favorable branch-level evidence. B23A then prospectively bounded stronger cross-family quantitative transfer claims. In a separate transfer programme, B24 identified multiple native boundary classes, B25 constructed a prospectively successful family-restricted transfer relation and reduced it to a fixed perimeter coefficient within a tested ellipse family, and B26 prospectively showed that the same frozen coefficient failed in a valid topology-straddling two-center test.

The retained programme-level conclusion is deliberately limited: BIG is better supported as a common formal language for system-dependent boundary geometry, response, history, branch structure, and reconfiguration than as one universal quantitative boundary law. The narrowing of the original universality hypothesis should not be read simply as a loss of scope. Successive prospective tests replaced a broad possibility with a more resolved map of what transfers, what remains family-dependent, and where transfer fails. In this sense, the programme became narrower in claim but more concrete in evidential content and more sharply testable.

The possibility of deeper universality is therefore not eliminated but reformulated. If a more general common law exists, the present evidence suggests that it may reside in relations, transformations, covariance rules, or branch-transition structure among distinct system-dependent boundary geometries rather than in one shared scalar, coefficient, or local law.
