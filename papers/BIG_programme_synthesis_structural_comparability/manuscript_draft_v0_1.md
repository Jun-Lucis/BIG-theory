**Jun Lucis**  
Independent Researcher

**Working manuscript draft v0.1 — 27 September 2026**  
**Not yet a formal publication**

## Abstract

Boundary Information Geometry (BIG) has developed through a sequence of reduced mathematical and numerical models in which boundaries are treated as active dynamical, geometric, historical, or response-generating structures. Early stages emphasized recurring local phenomena such as compact-like boundary landing, finite-time anisotropy thresholds, separation-like metastability, history-bearing interfaces, and boundary-dependent readout. Later stages asked a harder question: whether these recurring structures could be compressed into a small set of transferable quantitative laws.

This paper synthesizes the evidence from the later B19–B26 programme. B19 did not yield a robust ray-independent upstream scalar collapse in the tested synthetic vorticity families. The B19E external-validation branch subsequently found no prospectively established incremental predictive information from the frozen BIG additions beyond a strong baseline in its primary holdout, with (Delta R^2=-0.00285) and a hierarchical 95% interval spanning zero; an exact local-budget extension closed algebraically to numerical precision but did not rescue the predictive result. B20–B22 nevertheless established useful finite-resolution local response geometry within restricted frozen branches and subspaces, and B23 obtained favorable short-horizon branch-transfer evidence while retaining an inconclusive original aggregate verdict. B23A then prospectively tested stronger transfer claims across reconfiguration settings: a topology-sensitive piecewise/reset representation survived one free-boundary test, whereas unchanged smooth-normal transfer, stronger non-topological reset/locality claims, and a monotonic rate-ordering law were either not supported or numerically inconclusive. In a separate transfer programme, B24 found multiple native boundary classes rather than one already-existing common operator; B25 constructed a prospectively successful family-restricted transfer relation; and B26 then identified a finite-resolution geometry-transfer boundary of a fixed perimeter coefficient in a new two-center family.

The combined record supports a narrower interpretation of universality. BIG is better described here as a **common formal language for system-dependent boundary geometry, response, history, branch structure, and reconfiguration** than as one universal quantitative boundary law. The paper distinguishes structural, formal, and quantitative universality and treats negative and inconclusive prospective outcomes as claim-defining evidence rather than failed attempts to be retrospectively repaired.

## 1. Introduction

Boundary Information Geometry began from a broad organizing idea: boundaries need not be passive edges. In reduced models they can act as dynamical interfaces, geometric costs, carriers of retained history, readout structures, or zero sets generated by finite-time evolution and observation.

The early B-series established that this viewpoint can produce reproducible mathematical structure in several distinct model families. Examples include approximately quadratic compact-like boundary landing in degenerate mixed-gradient PDEs, finite-time anisotropy dependence, separation-like energy landscapes generated by boundary cost and nonlocal repulsion, moving free boundaries that retain and reuse history, and finite-window response boundaries with measurable local geometry.

Those results created an obvious but stronger question:

> Are the recurring structures manifestations of one transferable quantitative law, or are they better understood as distinct system-specific geometries written in a common formal language?

The later programme was designed increasingly around frozen protocols, held-out tests, explicit stopping rules, and retention of negative or inconclusive outcomes. This changes the evidential meaning of the series. A positive result in one family is not promoted automatically into a programme-wide law, and a failed or inconclusive transfer is not treated as a defect to be tuned away.

The central claim of this synthesis is therefore not that BIG has established a universal quantitative law. It is that the accumulated evidence supports a more limited and more defensible statement:

> **different systems can possess different boundary geometries while remaining comparable through a common formal architecture.**

This paper develops that statement through the evidence sequence B19–B26.

## 2. Three meanings of universality

The later BIG programme requires a separation between three levels that were easier to conflate in earlier exploratory work.

### 2.1 Structural universality

Structural universality refers to recurring organization in terms such as

[
	ext{boundary},quad
	ext{response},quad
	ext{history},quad
	ext{branch},quad
	ext{reconfiguration}.
]

This level does not require the same coefficients, equations, or local geometry in every system.

### 2.2 Formal universality

Formal universality means that different systems can be represented by comparable mathematical objects. Examples include a boundary field, a response zero set, a local normal, a Hessian, a history/readout map, a branch label, or a piecewise reconfiguration map.

A canonical later example is the finite-window response construction

[
F_T(P)=Z[U_T(P)]-Z[P],
]

with response boundary

[
mathcal B_T={P:F_T(P)=0}.
]

The same notation does not imply that the resulting geometry is numerically identical across systems.

### 2.3 Quantitative universality

Quantitative universality is the stronger claim that one low-dimensional numerical object transfers broadly: for example a scalar threshold, a normal field, a reset amplitude, a critical rate, or a fixed coefficient.

The B19–B26 programme increasingly tested this level prospectively.

The resulting evidence is asymmetric: formal comparability remains useful, while the strongest quantitative-transfer claims are repeatedly bounded by null, inconclusive, invalid, or negative results.

## 3. Background from B3–B18

The purpose of this section is not to re-review every earlier paper, but to state the recurring mathematical motifs that motivated the later universality tests.

### 3.1 Boundary formation and local landing

In the tested degenerate mixed-gradient PDE families, compact-like profiles repeatedly exhibit approximately quadratic local landing,

[
phi(s)sim As^
u,
qquad

uapprox2,
]

within the stated finite-resolution settings.

### 3.2 Shape-dependent finite-time fate

The B7–B8 sequence separated local boundary regularity from global finite-time fate. In the tested ellipse family, anisotropy shifts the finite-time survival/runaway threshold without eliminating the local boundary structure.

### 3.3 Boundary energy and reconfiguration

B9 introduced the reduced geometric energy

[
E(Omega;lambda)=sigma P(Omega)+lambda C(Omega),
]

where boundary cost competes with a nonlocal repulsive term. The model generates a compact branch, a finite deformation/pinch barrier, and a separated branch. The result is a reduced structural analogue of fission-like metastability, not a quantitative nuclear calculation.

### 3.4 History-bearing boundaries and readout

B14–B18 progressively separated stored history from dynamically readable history. The later readout construction emphasized that the effect of history can depend on current interface position, path exposure, and local accessibility rather than only on global stored mass.

These earlier results supplied the recurring vocabulary. They did not yet establish that one common quantitative law controlled all sectors.

## 4. B19: the limit of scalar reduction

B19 moved into synthetic divergence-free vorticity fields and asked whether finite-window amplification and decay could be reduced to one robust upstream scalar threshold.

The study combined boundary-core concentration measures, periodic Biot–Savart strain, signed strain alignment, high-vorticity gates, short-time viscous dynamics, and robustness controls. Under a reduced shrinking-core scaling argument, the stated stretching-versus-leakage assumptions yield a critical thickness exponent (s_*=5/4). This scaling result, however, did not become a universal dynamical classifier.

The numerical programme tested 21 single observables and eight predeclared two-coordinate portraits. None produced a robust ray-independent upstream collapse across the tested continuation directions.

Instead, the data were better described by distinct direction-dependent upstream channel families that feed into a common downstream production-versus-dissipation organization.

The important result is therefore negative and structural at the same time:

> **B19 does not identify one robust upstream scalar law, but it does identify a reproducible finite-family channel organization.**

This was the first strong indication that a common downstream mechanism need not imply a common low-dimensional upstream coordinate.

## 5. B19E: external predictive information and local-budget closure

The B19E branch asked whether BIG-style boundary/core descriptors add prospectively useful information in an independently generated flow setting beyond a strong baseline.

### 5.1 Frozen primary holdout

The audited primary holdout contained 1,536 rows from seeds 4 and 5. The frozen comparison used the same ridge regularization for the baseline and the augmented model.

The primary results were

[
R^2_{m baseline}=0.3285226,
]

[
R^2_{m augmented}=0.3256769,
]

and therefore

[
Delta R^2=-0.0028458.
]

The predeclared hierarchical bootstrap 95% interval was

[
[-0.0101698,;0.0042159].
]

Under the frozen rule, the final verdict was

[
oxed{	exttt{NULL_OR_INCONCLUSIVE}}.
]

The point estimate was slightly negative, but the confidence interval crossed zero. The result therefore does not establish incremental predictive information from the frozen BIG additions, and it is not promoted into a stronger negative-universality claim.

A uniform-only sensitivity check also gave a negative point estimate,

[
Delta R^2=-0.0046592.
]

No rescue analysis was used to alter the primary result.

### 5.2 Exact local-budget extension

A separate exact local-budget stage evaluated

[
dot Z_Omega=P-D+A+V_b+F+C_{m filt}.
]

Across 4,608 rows, the resolved budget closed to near machine precision under the frozen operator bookkeeping. The maximum relative closure error was approximately

[
8.44	imes10^{-16}.
]

This is a strong implementation and bookkeeping result, but not independent evidence for predictive universality.

At the primary control-volume scale, the simpler proxy (P-D) remained weak: sign agreement was 0.5625, median relative error approximately 0.852, and Spearman correlation with the reference rate approximately 0.159. The omitted correction was dominated by advective transport.

The exact closure therefore clarifies mechanism without rescuing the external predictive null:

> **algebraic closure of a local budget is not the same as incremental predictive universality.**

At the time of this draft, B19E is treated here as an audited programme result pending its own formal publication path.

## 6. B20–B22: local response geometry can still be predictive

The failure of a global scalar collapse did not eliminate useful geometry. B20 changed the object of study from an upstream scalar threshold to a finite-window response zero set.

### 6.1 B20: operator-generated response boundaries

Define

[
F_T(P)=Z[U_T(P)]-Z[P],
qquad
mathcal B_T={P:F_T(P)=0}.
]

B20.5 reconstructed local response gradients in a frozen normalized four-dimensional Family-C subspace. Cross-scale normal angles were on the order of (10^{-3}) degrees, and the primary prospective directional-derivative signs agreed in 24/24 cases.

B20.6 then attempted finite-distance crossing prediction. Only one of six independent frozen trajectories yielded an informative crossing in the predeclared domain. Its crossing orientation was correct, and the predicted location differed from the refined midpoint by approximately 1.66%.

Because the frozen criterion required more than one informative crossing, B20.6 remained

[
oxed{	exttt{INCONCLUSIVE}}.
]

This is an example of a local success that is intentionally not promoted into a broader crossing law.

### 6.2 B21: local kinematic closure

B21 tested the transport identity

[
rac{Dg}{dT}
=
partial_T g+H_Sdot P_B
]

along a tracked response-boundary branch.

In the terminal prospective test, the closure residual was approximately 0.124%, below the first-order root-bracket uncertainty estimate of approximately 0.217%. No reproducible residual requiring an additional effective boundary-dynamical term was resolved.

The result is local numerical kinematic closure, not a new boundary equation.

### 6.3 B22: second-order prospective geometry

B22 extended the local construction to Hessian, curvature, normal rotation, and principal directions. On a reserved branch, two sequential held-out short-horizon forecasts passed all frozen gates.

The key point is conceptual:

> **local finite-resolution geometry can be prospectively useful even when a programme-wide scalar law is absent.**

## 7. B23: favorable branch transfer without retrospective upgrading

B23 asked whether the B22 short-horizon construction transfers across multiple branches.

Across four branches and three ray directions, the validly evaluated position/normal forecasts met their declared gates, and two designated full-geometry sentinels also passed Hessian, principal-curvature, and principal-direction criteria.

However, the original aggregate plan could not be executed exactly for two endpoint-near targets because the symmetric search-window protocol was not feasible there.

The retained classification is therefore not a global success label. The original aggregate remains

[
oxed{	exttt{INCONCLUSIVE}}.
]

Supplementary repaired runs are not counted retroactively as successes of the original frozen plan.

This preservation rule becomes important for the later universality audit.

## 8. B23A: prospective audit of structural versus quantitative universality

B23A explicitly separated structural/formal universality from quantitative universality and closed its own P1–P6 branch without a rescue stage.

### 8.1 P1: unchanged smooth-normal transfer

A new B9 reconfiguration family was used to test whether the previously successful smooth local-normal geometry transferred unchanged.

The retained verdict was

[
	exttt{NOT_SUPPORTED_AT_NEW_B9_RECONFIGURATION_FAMILY}.
]

Thus formal portability of the construction did not imply quantitative portability of the normal field.

### 8.2 P2B: branch orientation inside B9

A third B9 family showed the same connected-versus-separated branch orientation pattern under the frozen internal test.

This was retained as support for a **B9-internal branch structure**, not a coordinate-free universal law.

### 8.3 P3: topology-sensitive piecewise/reset representation

A one-dimensional free-boundary merger prospectively supported a reset contrast for the frozen threshold-support geometry vector.

However, a post-hoc outer-boundary-only diagnostic was consistent with zero. The retained interpretation is therefore narrower than a universal reset law:

> piecewise/reset representation is supported for the frozen topology-sensitive support geometry.

### 8.4 P4 and P5: stronger non-topological reset/locality claims

P4 removed the directly topology-sensitive target but became resolution-sensitive. P5 replaced that target with a finite-window local response susceptibility and again failed to produce a stable claim-bearing locality reset across resolution.

Both remain

[
oxed{	exttt{INCONCLUSIVE_NUMERICAL_SENSITIVITY}}.
]

### 8.5 P6: prospective rate ordering

P6 prospectively tested the post-hoc P5 rate trend on nine new midpoint speeds.

At (N=256),

]

At (N=320),

## 9. B24–B26: from multiple boundary classes to a bounded transfer relation

B24–B26 form a separate transfer programme. They ask not whether one native operator already exists everywhere, but whether distinct boundary classes can be connected constructively.

### 9.1 B24: no single pre-existing substantive operator

A source-audited mapping across four frozen sectors did not identify the required exact or coordinate-equivalent substantive recurrence.

The retained verdict was

[
oxed{	exttt{MULTIPLE_BOUNDARY_CLASSES_INDICATED}}.
]

This does not invalidate earlier results. It limits the hypothesis that they were already instances of one common operator.

### 9.2 B25 Phase I: failed simple bridge, successful level-resolved bridge

The first constant-density reduction,

The result

[

A retrospective coarea decomposition then motivated a new predictor. Crucially, the replacement was frozen before new trajectories:

On nine new held-out shape/resolution cases, all were valid. The maximum relative error was 17.76%, the median 9.49%, and fine-resolution stability passed.

The formal verdict was

[
oxed{	exttt{AB_LEVEL_RESOLVED_BRIDGE_PASS}}.
]

## 10. Synthesis: what survives?

The combined B19–B26 record supports several distinctions.

### 10.1 Local predictability is not global universality

B20–B22 demonstrate that a local response boundary can possess numerically stable and prospectively useful geometry. B23 shows that some of this construction transfers across branches.

But B23A shows that this does not imply one unchanged smooth-normal field, one reset amplitude, one locality contrast, or one rate law across reconfiguration families.

### 10.2 A successful family-restricted coefficient need not be universal

B25.1e provides a strong prospective fixed-coefficient result inside the held-out ellipse family. B26 then gives a valid prospective failure in a new two-center family.

The two results are not contradictory. Together they locate the domain of validity.

### 10.3 Exact closure is not predictive sufficiency

The B19E local budget closes algebraically to near machine precision, yet the simpler production-minus-dissipation proxy remains weak at the tested local scales, and the frozen primary external predictive increment remains null/inconclusive.

A correctly closed identity can therefore be mechanistically informative without being a sufficient low-dimensional predictor.

### 10.4 Branch identity and reconfiguration must be explicit

Across B9, B20–B23A, and B26, branch identity repeatedly matters. Piecewise local geometry is more compatible with the evidence than a single globally smooth low-dimensional surface spanning all regimes.

### 10.5 The natural universal object may be a transformation rule rather than a number

The strongest remaining programme-level question is no longer whether one coefficient appears everywhere. It is whether system-specific boundary geometries obey common transformation, covariance, composition, or normal-form rules.

This shifts the search for universality from

[
	ext{same scalar everywhere}
]

toward

[
	ext{same relation between system-dependent geometries}.
]

## 11. Claim boundaries

This synthesis does not establish:

- one universal BIG equation;
- one universal boundary scalar, normal field, reset amplitude, or rate law;
- a global invariant manifold;
- a continuum free-boundary or curvature theorem;
- a Navier–Stokes blow-up, regularity, or singularity result;
- quantitative nuclear-fission or nuclear-fusion theory;
- biological, cognitive, AI, or cosmological validity;
- programme-wide external empirical validation.

The phrase **structural universality** is used here only as a working description of recurring organization. It is not a theorem that all systems possess the same boundary structure.

The stronger retained programme-level formulation is:

> **a common formal language for system-dependent boundary geometry.**

---

## 12. Open problems

The next theoretical questions are now more sharply defined.

### 12.1 Transformation and covariance laws

Can boundary-response objects be related under explicit coordinate, scale, topology, or representation changes?

### 12.2 Branch-local invariants

Which quantities remain stable on a branch even when cross-branch quantitative transfer fails?

### 12.3 Piecewise normal forms

Can reconfiguration events be represented by a small grammar of local geometric pieces plus transition maps?

### 12.4 Continuum control

Which finite-resolution findings admit a controlled continuum limit, and which are intrinsically numerical observables?

### 12.5 External validation

Which structural or formal BIG objects survive domain-specific equations, units, calibration, and empirical comparison?

The B19E result makes this last question especially important: external validation should test incremental information beyond strong established baselines rather than merely reproduce an internal BIG coordinate.

---

## 13. Conclusion

The B19–B26 sequence changes the interpretation of Boundary Information Geometry.

The programme does not support a simple progression toward one universal scalar law. Instead, it reveals a layered structure:

[
	ext{local reproducible geometry}
]

[
+quad
	ext{prospectively useful family-restricted reductions}
]

[
+quad
	ext{explicit branch/reconfiguration dependence}
]

[
+quad
	ext{prospectively identified transfer limits}.
]

The resulting picture is narrower but more testable.

A useful final statement is:

> **The same geometry need not occur everywhere; the same rules for representing, comparing, and transforming system-dependent geometries may be the more plausible universal object.**

In this sense, BIG is currently better supported as a formalism for **difference-preserving comparability** than as a theory of one universal quantitative boundary law.
